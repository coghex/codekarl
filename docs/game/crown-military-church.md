# Codekarl vision: Crown, military, church and history

Part of [the vision](../vision.md), moved verbatim from it on 2026-10-21. Owner decisions are marked **Firm** with their date; everything else is **Open** or a **Proposal**, as there.

## Crown, military, church and history

- **Firm.** Early military forces rely on levies. Each population group's contribution is calculated under the country's laws, which the
  player can change. Standing armies become feasible as revenue grows. Ships are expensive and take years to build.
- **Firm.** Crown spending includes armies and fleets, the upkeep of castles, holds and garrisons, directly managed crown property, minor royal
  expenditure, bribes, and diplomacy. Infrastructure, education and similar spending are possible but optional, especially early on.
- **Firm.** The church matters. Playing the Papal States should offer many distinctive options. **Open:** the institutional accounting and
  control.
  - **Corrected (owner, 2026-10-18 03:04Z).** The earlier statement that local parishes distribute revenue to the crown and to the pope was
    a misstatement. Clergy tithe to the pope if they are the right type of Catholic. In the Irish example the clergy were Insular, and the
    owner believes they did not tithe to the pope (needs research). See "Authority, ownership, laws and taxation" below.
- *Example (desired interacting behaviour; historical chronology needs research):* in Ireland at the proposed start there is no coinage, so
  barter must be modelled. Vikings later introduce coinage, mining begins, taxes are taken, and monasteries fill with coin and grain. Then
  the Vikings return and seize those stores.

### Levies, equipment and army supply (owner, 2026-10-18 15:31Z, 15:46Z and 15:48Z)

Answers to ck-guide's three questions of 15:19Z (at 15:31Z), its three of 15:32Z (at 15:46Z) and its two of 15:47Z (at 15:48Z).

- **Firm. Levies leave their jobs but do not become soldier pops.** Soldier pops are the standing-army pops employed in barracks, castles,
  garrisons and armories.
- **Firm. Each location levies a number of people,** based on various factors. That population is marked with how many levies are
  currently raised from it.
- **Firm. The pop type determines the kind of levy.** Peasants become peasant levies and nobles become knights; clergy as well (the owner's
  example: plated holy knights). This would differ for different cultures and technologies; "there are lots of things we could do with a
  system like this".
  - These are flavour examples, not historical claims or a settled list of unit types.
- **Firm. Raising levies takes workers from their jobs.** *Owner's example:* from a farm employing 100 farmers, say 10 are taken, and the
  farm then slowly rehires from the pop's dependents or unemployed.
- **Firm. Disbanded levies join the unemployed,** and always retain their original identity.
- **Firm. Equipment depends on technology and laws.** Professional troops should be supplied by the crown; levies buy their own equipment.
  - *Owner's examples:* for a peasant, this could mean buying a single tool, such as a farming implement used as a weapon, "common in
    medieval times"; a noble may require plated armour, professionally crafted weapons and a horse. "Again, a lot of flavour can come
    here."
- **Firm. Armies have a food level.** Regular levies each carry a small amount of food.
  - **Firm (the owner's "i think").** A percentage of the peasants is levied as "supply caravans", providing little combat ability but
    carrying a large amount of food.
- **Firm. Raising levies creates a mobilisation job.** When the king raises levies, a job is created in each location, like a one-off
  building. It requires all the necessary supplies, food and weapons, and all of it is paid for by the king.
- **Firm (owner, 15:46Z). How the two payment statements fit.** It depends on the laws, the technology and who is being raised. A levied
  pop first grabs any weapons it has in its own stockpile, according to the levy-raising building's production method. Any remaining
  supplies necessary then appear as orders to be filled, and the crown has to pay for them.
  - The crown owns the levy-raising building, so the player can set its production method directly (as for any crown-owned building).
- **Firm (owner, 15:46Z). The method sets the equipment standard.** The player should be able to select steel weapons, but also tools, or
  perhaps an option like "whatever's available", which raises a mix of well-armed and poorly armed peasants.
  - Mobilisation waits for supplies: the owner answered "yes, based on the production method" to ck-guide's question whether it does. The
    owner then wrote "if these requirements can be fulfilled, then the levies stay stuck waiting"; *ck-guide's 15:46Z reading (not
    confirmed by the owner)* is "cannot", which fits the "yes".
  - The player should be able to cancel mobilisation early, releasing whatever partial levies are available from that location.
- **Replaced (owner, 15:48Z; see the food policy below).** *Earlier (15:31Z):* when an army captures a location, it takes any food in
  stockpile and stores it in the army's supplies.
- **Firm (the owner's ideal, 15:46Z). An army food policy.** A small selection in the army's panel, defaulting to buying food: the king
  then pays for supplies from the nearest location. It can be set to seize food instead: nothing is paid, but the satisfaction of the
  affected pops decreases, and they may starve if they do not have enough stockpiled.
  - **Firm (owner, 15:48Z). Captured locations behave just like owned locations** with respect to feeding soldiers, so the army's buy or
    seize policy applies there too, replacing the automatic taking of food on capture.
- **Firm (owner, 15:48Z: "its probably best"). Contributions return on disbanding.** How much supply each pop brings to the levy is noted
  in advance. When the levy is done, that is returned to the pop's stockpile, and any overflow is sold. The rest is divided among the
  crown's collective stores, in keeps, barracks, armories and so on.
  - *ck-guide's 15:49Z qualification (not an owner decision):* this applies to goods that actually remain; consumed food and lost weapons
    cannot be returned.
- **Open:** how losses are divided between contributing pops and the crown; what overflow means, and who buys it; the capacity of crown
  stores; who owns seized food; army food capacity and what happens to any excess; whether the nearest location can supply what is bought,
  and how it travels; how long mobilisation takes, and when the raised people are counted as levied; the factors that decide how many people
  a location levies, and what share become supply caravans; how combat losses are counted against the source pop; and reinforcement.

### Battles, garrisons and control (owner, 2026-10-18 15:59Z, 16:10Z, 16:17Z and 16:20Z)

Answers to ck-guide's three questions of 15:49Z (at 15:59Z), its three of 15:59Z (at 16:10Z) its two of 16:11Z (at 16:17Z) and its one of
16:18Z (at 16:20Z). At 16:10Z the owner opened with "ok that sounds right", then clarified ck-guide's remark on territorial control (below).

- **Firm. Army movement is a map discussion,** reserved for later; the owner is "still not sure how i want to do it". Nothing about
  movement or routes is decided here.
- **Firm. In battle, the only player choice is the army commander.** That gets into the character system: characters have traits that
  determine their tactics, and the player can put the right generals in charge to get the results they want.
- **Firm. Combat plays itself out, hour by hour,** until one side has no units left, retreats, or, most commonly, breaks from morale.
- **Firm. Garrisons are control.** Each location has a garrison by default; even a single lone farm should have a small one (*owner's
  example:* maybe 5-10 men).
  - A garrison requires a small amount of supplies to maintain.
  - A big castle in a city with city walls may have thousands in its garrison. They are added to any battle at that location if the
    "location owner" is involved in the battle (ck-manager's reading: the tag controlling the location, since locations have no
    private owner).
- **Firm. Capturing a location replaces its garrison.** The owner: "if we capture an enemy location, we should remove the garrison from the
  numbers of raised levies, then fill the location with our new garrison."
  - **Firm (owner, 16:10Z: "yes, that is what i was trying to say").** The occupying garrison comes directly out of the capturing army: in
    ck-guide's example, an army of 1,000 captures a location, leaves 20 behind and departs with 980. Those 20 remain unavailable to their
    home pops until released from garrison duty.
  - **Firm (owner, 16:10Z).** When a location is captured and its garrison refilled from the player's army, that garrison switches
    ownership; on peace, ownership reverses. (This clarified ck-guide's 15:59Z remark that installing a garrison changes who controls the
    location without transferring its privately owned farms and workshops. The owner did not address those businesses.)
- **Firm. On peace, if the land is not taken,** the occupying garrison comes home, and the "original owner" has to refill its garrison.
- **Firm (owner, 16:10Z). Normal garrisons are soldier pops,** hired from peasants or other local pops. The wages are better than being a
  basic labourer or peasant, so a garrison hires quickly.
  - The owner: "the building owner pays the fee, but garrisons should mostly be owned by the crown i think." **Open:** whether "the building
    owner" is the garrison's own owner, as the rest of the sentence suggests, or the owners of buildings at the location, as ck-guide's
    question put it.
- **Firm (owner, 16:10Z: "im thinking"). Private garrisons.** Garrisons can also be privately owned, and a privately held defensive
  structure can hold a private garrison alongside the natural crown garrison.
  - *Owner's examples:* a wealthy Italian bank may choose to build a fort and hire its own garrison if it is in a weak and unprotected city;
    in more rural areas, monasteries or tribesmen may own their own garrisons separate from the crown, perhaps even in locations that have
    no crown garrison.
  - **Firm (owner, 16:17Z). Private garrisons fight for the de jure owner of the location,** "as in the crown". The owner is "thinking 90%"
    of the time this will be the defender, but if the town is occupied, they join the side of the attacker.
- **Firm. Taking a location.** If the garrison is empty, capture is immediate. Otherwise the attackers have to drain the garrison, with a
  percentage chance during the process that the garrison simply surrenders ("white flag") and is captured.
  - **Firm (owner, 16:17Z). All garrisons in a location are treated as one unit, having joined forces.** The owner reminded that the
    garrison should break at some point and surrender before defeating every hostile is necessary, so an attacker does not have to defeat
    every defender.
  - **Firm (owner, 16:20Z: "yes that is right"). After a capture, no original garrison remains.** All the surviving garrison become
    prisoner soldier pops, and the occupier fills all garrisons, even private ones (from its own troops, as above).
  - **Firm (owner, 16:17Z). Relieving a siege.** If an army attacks to relieve a siege by an enemy, that army is treated as the defender in
    the fight, and the remaining garrison joins the defenders.
- **Firm. Captured garrisons are treated like captured armies.** They become a new unit, merged into the army that captured them, marked
  with an icon in the army list as prisoners of war.
  - **Firm (owner, 16:10Z).** There should be little buttons on the unit to ransom, release, hire and execute prisoners.
- Control of a location is separate from the ownership of its buildings by pops (see "Authority, ownership, laws and taxation").
- **Open:** how control works when a location has several garrisons; what happens to occupied garrisons when the land is taken at peace;
  garrison wage levels and supplies; how draining a garrison works (siege or assault), and the surrender chance; casualties and the morale
  model; the costs, conditions and consequences of the prisoner actions, and what happens to prisoners otherwise; and the commander traits
  and tactics themselves.

### Characters (owner, 2026-10-18 16:33Z, 16:44Z and 17:22Z)

Answers to ck-guide's three questions of 16:21Z (at 16:33Z), its three of 16:34Z (at 16:44Z) and its two of 16:45Z (at 17:22Z).

- **Firm (the owner's "i think"). Each location gets at least one character.** It could be a mayor; rulers and generals for sure.
  - *Owner's examples:* monasteries should have an abbot; holds can have lords, such as dukes or barons.
- **Firm. Offices can be cultural and religious.** *Owner's examples:* Catholics will have the pope, Muslims will have a mufti,
  Anglo-Saxons could have sheriffs, and steppe hordes could have warlords. The owner wants a way to represent all of these.
  - These are illustrative content needing research, not a settled list of offices or an equivalence between them.
- **Firm. A characters interface.** There should be an interface to quickly search and see characters.
  - *ck-guide's 16:34Z suggestion (not an owner decision):* one searchable roster, filtered by location, office, culture, religion and
    skills; opened from an army, it could highlight military abilities without hiding the king or other civilian officeholders.
- **Firm. Each country has a pool of characters.** The player can click on an army to select one of them to lead it. It need not be a
  commander: the player should be able to have their king lead their army, for example.
- **Firm. "Promote commander".** At the end of that list is a button, "promote commander", which generates a commander for the player for a
  small fee. The new commander's talent derives from the skill and experience of the army it is promoted into, alongside certain cultural
  and technology factors.
- **Firm. Skills are levels.** Each character has a number of skills, each like a level: level 1, level 2 and so on, with no limit, though
  they should not get much higher than 10. The experience needed to level up increases exponentially.
- **Firm. Traits.** Characters have traits, not all of them combat-related, that affect various aspects in and out of battle.
  - The owner called ck-guide's 16:21Z examples good: cautious commanders retreating earlier, aggressive ones pursuing broken enemies, and
    disciplined ones conserving supplies. There are many more.
- **Firm (owner, 16:44Z). Every character is attached to a pop.** A commander promoted from a peasant levy is still a peasant. Pops can
  have several characters attached at once.
- **Firm (owner, 16:44Z). Characters do not own property or wealth; their pop does.**
  - *Owner's example:* if an abbot dies, the abbot's clergy pop automatically promotes a new clergyman, with stats derived from that pop.
- **Firm (owner, 16:44Z). Families and succession run in the background,** not "so much in the player's face".
  - A king who is of age should find a spouse, and one is generated if no suitable characters are found. Children are generated
    automatically.
  - If a king dies without an heir, succession follows the country's succession law; each country has a law for this.
  - *Owner's example (the owner's belief, needing research for the chosen start date):* France will start with Salic law, so the crown
    would pass to the nearest male relative in the succession. This means simulating full family trees for each noble family.
  - No heir at all results in an election for a new heir, inviting a local family or a foreign royal family to the crown. This could
    trigger a political crisis, or perhaps war, if some pops have radicalism.
- **Firm (owner, 16:44Z). Local officeholders have skills and traits,** just like commanders. Their administrative skills increase over
  time with successful rule, and their traits can do things like increase production or lower unrest, or have negative effects.
- **Firm (owner, 17:22Z). Laws determine appointments.** Whether the crown can replace local officeholders (ck-guide asked about mayors,
  abbots and local lords) is set by the laws, and initially it cannot in most countries. This does not change the player's choice of army
  commanders (above).
- **Firm (owner, 17:22Z). All traits and skills are visible.** The owner first answered "yes" to ck-guide's 16:45Z question, which bundled
  full visibility with ck-guide's suggestion that some personal traits might stay hidden; asked to clarify, the owner said "all traits and
  skill visible": exact skill levels and all traits are shown immediately.
- **Open:** which skills exist and how experience is gained; who is eligible for which offices, which laws grant which appointment rights,
  and whether one character can hold several offices or commands; how a replacement's stats are derived from its pop; how families relate
  across pops and countries; how death and ageing work; the election process; what counts as successful rule; the promotion fee and formula;
  and the trait list and effects.

### Authority, ownership, laws and taxation (owner, 2026-10-18 03:04Z, 03:14Z and 03:30Z)

- **Firm. Only buildings and agriculture have owners.** Land and locations have no private owner. The owner said both that most land is
  barren and "will be owned by nobody for now", and that land ownership "is considered to be all owned by the state".
  - **Open:** the exact distinction between unowned and state-owned land. No land titles or land property mechanics are introduced.
- **Firm. Who owns what.**
  - The king might own a few businesses around town: the keep, perhaps an armory, and similar things.
  - A farm could be owned by a peasant pop. That pop pays no tax to any lord, unless the king levies a tax over the land.
  - Typically a noble owns the farm. The noble takes the outputs and pays the peasants a wage, in coin or in goods, based on the farm's
    output. The peasants should be able to survive on what the nobles pay them, but the large share goes to keep the nobles rich.
  - A monastery location can have buildings owned by different groups: the clergy can own the monastery while a noble owns the attached
    farm, or the clergy can own both.
  - Workers are defined by their jobs. A monastery's employees are mostly clergy; one clergy pop owns the monastery, and other clergy pops may
    just live and work there.
- **Firm. Laws** depend on the political system. They must be easy to change: "the worst part of the Victoria games are the laws, we should
  approach the problem more intelligently."
  - The political system is itself a law, so laws affect the behaviour of other laws.
  - There should be culture- and religion-specific laws.
  - **Open:** how laws are enacted under each political system.
  - *ck-guide's 02:44Z suggestion (not an owner decision):* the legal authority to issue an order and the practical ability to enforce it can
    differ.
- **Firm. Taxation depends on the law.** Late in the game, taxation should be represented as the government taking a percentage of goods from
  different pops. Early in the game the law is more feudal, and taxes are collected by the lords going up the chain:
  - monasteries collect taxes on the farms and buildings they own, and stay tax-exempt until the law changes, so they become incredibly
    wealthy;
  - nobles tax their serfs, then pay their tax to the king; merchants work the same way. Only the clergy are tax-exempt.
  - The crown directly taxes certain pops, such as merchants and nobles. Commoners are taxed by the owners of their buildings, as
    intermediaries. Buildings owned directly by the crown provide their full revenue after costs and wages.
  - Monasteries are repositories of wealth, acquiring "more and more lands outside of the direct crown control". Since only buildings and
    agriculture have owners, this is read as growing holdings of buildings and farms. **Open:** confirm that reading with the owner.
- **Firm. No full feudal simulation.** A noble pop group can represent barons, dukes or other lords without specifying which; all that
  matters is that there is an intermediary. (ck-guide's question about separate vassal tags was a misunderstanding: the owner meant only who
  owns the land.)
- **Firm (owner, 03:14Z). Owners keep the output and pay wages.** The owner collects the building's outputs and pays the workers a wage
  determined by productivity and profit. This answers ck-guide's 03:05Z question: the owner's retained share is itself the feudal
  extraction, and the owner does not levy a second tax on the workers' wages. (In ck-guide's illustration, a noble-owned farm producing
  100 sacks of barley uses 20 for operating costs, pays workers 30 and keeps 50, which the crown can then tax.)
  - The exception is buildings owned by their workers, such as subsistence farms: those workers pay a wage to themselves, keeping all the
    outputs, and pay any additional farmhands who are not owners.
- **Firm. A bad harvest affects both owner and wage worker.** If that makes the worker starve, so be it: the owner of a building is under no
  obligation to pay the peasants anything.
  - A wealthy owner with a lot of excess might cover the loss for a few cycles. That is a later design, to be decided then.
- **Firm. A law change takes effect immediately.** *Example:* the king removes the clergy's tax exemption. It takes effect at once; clergy
  satisfaction drops, political radicalism rises, and the clergy begin to foment a political movement.
- **Firm (owner, building on ck-guide's 03:05Z idea). Tax refusal.** A pop in a political movement related to taxation does not pay the taxes
  when its radicalism is above a certain level. In the example, once the tax changes and the movement is triggered, the clergy's
  satisfaction and radicalism can be such that they refuse to pay. The player is notified that pops are refusing taxation.
  - Refusal comes from membership in a tax-related movement combined with radicalism above the threshold, not from membership alone, and
    not every tax change necessarily drives every pop to refuse.
  - **Firm (owner, 03:30Z), for now:** the king cannot do anything about refusal except stop the law. No negotiation, forced collection or
    other remedy is added at this stage. The movement's existing behaviour on concession still applies (it is dismissed, and its agitation
    returns, fading slowly, if the law is changed back).
  - **Firm (owner).** Unpaid taxes do not become a debt: missed taxes are lost revenue.
  - **Owner's preference (03:30Z, "refuse all taxes I think"):** pops that qualify refuse all taxes, not only the disputed one. Refusing
    taxes is all that is recorded; nothing here extends refusal to wages or commercial payments.
  - *ck-guide's 03:15Z suggestions (not owner decisions):* show refusal in the treasury breakdown (expected tax, tax received, and which pops
    withheld the difference), and have the movement screen explain why pops refuse and what would restore compliance. ck-guide also
    suggested refusal could escalate from the disputed tax to all taxes with radicalism; the owner's preference above is all taxes.
- **Open:** how the levied amounts flow without counting the same goods twice; the refusal threshold; exactly which payments count as taxes
  for refusal (for example, taxes owed to the crown or to intermediaries), and how this interacts with overlapping movements; when refusal
  stops and payment resumes; how refusal by an intermediary passes along the tax chain; and the details of enacting laws.
